Keep it simple: · Three pillars, three ETFs, low correlation.

Nine Frankfurt-listed stocks grouped into three equal-weighted ETFs, one per investment pillar, and why combining the pillars diversifies a portfolio.

Portfolio rationale

The portfolio is built from three investment pillars: Banking, Industry, and Oil & Energy. For each pillar I build a small equal-weighted ETF of three stocks, so each stock is one third of its pillar ETF. Holding the three ETFs together gives nine stocks, one ninth each.

The aim is to show that the pillars move remarkably independently of each other, even though all nine stocks trade on the Frankfurt Stock Exchange in euros. Stocks within one pillar share the same economic drivers and tend to move together. The pillars themselves respond to different forces, so combining the three ETFs lowers the risk of the whole portfolio without giving up equity exposure.

Banking ETF 3 stocks

  • Interest rates and the yield curve, which set lending margins
  • The credit cycle and loan losses
  • Regulation, capital returns and consolidation

Industry ETF 3 stocks

  • Corporate investment cycles and order books
  • Grid, wind and power equipment for the energy transition
  • Project execution and industrial services demand

Oil & Energy ETF 3 stocks

  • Crude prices and refining margins
  • Power prices and renewable generation
  • Geopolitics and supply shocks

An oil price spike lifts oil producers but raises costs for industrial companies; a change in interest rates moves banks far more than turbine makers or utilities. Mixing integrated oil with a renewable power producer inside the Oil & Energy pillar also spreads its exposure across fossil and green energy. Because these shocks hit the pillars differently, their returns partly offset each other.

The idea in one sentence

Own the three sectors the real economy cannot run without, the ones that finance it, build it and power it, through companies whose value rests on today's revenues, and let their different cycles offset each other.

1 · The backbone of the economy

Every economic activity needs credit, physical capacity and energy. Households and firms can delay a new phone or a holiday; they cannot stop using payment systems, maintaining plants or buying power. The three pillars sit at those choke points.

Banking: finances the economy

Moves savings into loans, runs payments, underwrites trade and investment. No sector can grow without credit.

Revenue engine
Net interest income on loans and deposits, fees from payments, asset management and capital markets.
Demand driver
Loan growth, the level and shape of interest rates, business and trade activity.
Structural tailwind
Positive interest rates after a decade of zero rates; consolidation and buybacks in European banking; financing Europe's investment wave.
Watch
Net interest margin, CET1 capital ratio, cost of risk, cost/income ratio.

Industry: builds the economy

Makes and maintains the physical capital: power plants, grids, turbines, process plants, industrial services.

Revenue engine
Order backlog converted into sales over several years, plus recurring service and maintenance contracts.
Demand driver
Corporate and public investment budgets, the energy transition, electrification.
Structural tailwind
Germany's €500 bn infrastructure and climate-neutrality fund (approved March 2025, over 12 years); the EU's estimate of €584 bn grid investment needed this decade.
Watch
Order intake, book-to-bill, backlog, service share of revenue, margins and free cash flow.

Oil & Energy: powers the economy

Supplies the fuels and electricity everything else runs on, from fossil (integrated oil, refining) to renewable generation.

Revenue engine
Sales of crude, gas, refined products and power; long-term power purchase agreements and regulated returns.
Demand driver
Energy consumption, commodity and power prices, refining margins.
Structural tailwind
Rising electricity demand from electrification and data centres; energy security after 2022. Mixing oil with renewables spreads the pillar across both energy worlds.
Watch
Oil and gas prices, refining margins, power prices, production volumes, capex discipline and dividends.

2 · Why "revenue-driven" matters

What the pillars have in common

  • Essential demand. Credit, industrial maintenance and energy are needed in every phase of the cycle; volumes fall less than for discretionary goods.
  • Visible, often contracted revenue. Loan books, multi-year order backlogs, service contracts and power purchase agreements give earnings visibility.
  • Value anchored in current earnings. Much of the share price is backed by profits and dividends being earned now, not by profits expected in ten years. That makes the stocks less sensitive to rising discount rates than long-duration growth stocks.
  • Inflation pass-through. Banks earn more when rates rise with inflation, energy companies sell at market prices, and industrial contracts are often index-linked.
  • Cash returns. Mature, cash-generative businesses tend to pay dividends and buy back shares, which adds a steady component to total return.

The honest caveat

Revenue-driven does not mean defensive. All three pillars are cyclical: bank profits fall in recessions as loan losses rise, industrial orders get postponed when clients delay investment, and energy earnings swing with commodity prices.

The strategy does not rely on any single pillar being safe. It relies on the pillars being exposed to different cycles, so that a bad year for one is rarely a bad year for all three at once. Sections 3 and 4 test that idea.

4 · Systematic risk: what diversification can and cannot do

The theory in three lines

A stock's risk has two parts. Idiosyncratic risk comes from the company or its sector (a profit warning, a regulation, an oil-price move). Systematic risk comes from the whole market (a recession, a global sell-off) and can never be fully diversified away inside equities.

Combining three pillars with different drivers removes most of the idiosyncratic and sector risk and lowers the exposure to systematic risk, because one pillar (here Oil & Energy) barely moves with the broad market. What remains is a portfolio that still falls in a market crash, but by less.

5 · Risks to the thesis

  • Home bias. Most of the companies are German or European, so a European recession, energy crisis or political shock would hit all three pillars together.
  • Value and cyclical tilt. All three pillars are "old economy" value sectors. In a growth-led or technology-driven market they can lag for long periods, and the portfolio has no technology, healthcare or consumer-staples exposure.
  • Policy and regulation. Bank capital rules, windfall taxes on energy, subsidy changes for renewables and wind, and carbon pricing can change profitability quickly.
  • Energy transition. Oil assets risk becoming stranded over time; renewables and wind manufacturers face execution and pricing pressure. Holding both reduces but does not remove this.
  • Concentration. Nine stocks is a small portfolio; company events (profit warnings, takeovers, mergers) still move the result.
  • Correlations are not constant. They rose in the April 2025 sell-off and will rise again in the next crisis (see the chart above and the stress tests in the Short-term risk tab).
  • Long history is less kind. Over 20 years the 1-year correlation between the pillars was around 0.4–0.5 most of the time, the two pairs involving Oil & Energy are now near their lowest readings since 2001, and the portfolio fell more than the DAX in 7 of the 10 crises since 2001, all of them bank-, credit- or liquidity-driven (see Long-term risk and Crisis history).

6 · How to run it

Implementation

  • Hold the three pillar ETFs in equal parts (or a risk-parity mix; see the Short-term risk tab and the Crisis history implementation section) and rebalance back to target, for example quarterly or when a pillar drifts more than 5 points.
  • Keep each stock near one third of its pillar so no single company dominates.
  • Set a risk budget, such as a 1-day 99% Expected Shortfall limit, and review positions when it is breached.
  • Replace a stock when its business no longer fits its pillar's role, not because of short-term price moves.

Data notes

  • Daily dividend-adjusted closes from Yahoo Finance on each stock's primary listing (Xetra, Euronext Paris and Amsterdam, Warsaw converted to EUR), aligned to the Xetra calendar; other venues' holidays are filled with the last price for up to five days.
  • A stock joins its pillar on its first reliable price: Industry 1 from April 2001, Oil & Energy 3 from April 2003, Oil & Energy 1 from July 2005 (earlier data is not adjusted for a share unification), Industry 3 from September 2020 (listing date). Before that the pillar ETF is the equal-weighted average of the members that existed, so pillar risk is measured with two stocks in those years.
  • These primary-listing prices close at the same time as the DAX, so betas and correlations here are more accurate than those from the Frankfurt floor quotes used in the other tabs. One series differs materially: Frankfurt quotes for Oil & Energy 3 before mid-2025 are far below the primary listing converted to EUR, which overstates that stock's two-year return in the short-term data.
  • Overlapping holding periods are not independent: 20 years contain only about 20 separate 1-year periods and 4 separate 5-year periods, so tail estimates at 99% and above rest on very few real episodes. The bootstrap and lognormal estimates extrapolate; read them as orders of magnitude.

Data: daily closing prices of nine stocks listed on the Frankfurt Stock Exchange, October 2024 – October 2026 (overview, volatility, correlation, thesis and short-term risk tabs), and daily prices of the same stocks on their primary listings since 2000 (long-term risk and crisis history), sourced from Yahoo Finance. Company names are anonymised and prices are rebased to 100, so only relative moves are shown. Pillar ETFs are hypothetical equal-weighted baskets rebalanced daily, before costs. Weeks with fewer than three trading days are excluded from the volatility figures. One Oil & Energy stock trades thinly on this venue, so isolated stale quotes can lift its weekly volatility.
